Top 3 Resources:

Below, you can find my top 3 resources for A-Level Film Studies revision. These are resources I found through research as I did not take Film Studies at A-Level myself. You may find other resources that are more useful for your revision!

1. Your exam board website

Your exam board's website is best for knowing what you're actually being examined on. Use your specification, past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports. AQA usually provides example student answers with examiner commentaries too.

2. BFI Education

BFI Education is one of the best specialist resources for Film Studies. Its educational materials include close analysis, critical theory, national cinema, representation and film context. This time, the link will lead to the Study Day for A-Level Film Studies, which is a fantastic resource for revision. It "evaluate and apply relevant critical readings in order to engage with the issues and debates referenced in all the A Level specifications". The day is usually in March and costs £12.50 per student, but your school may be able to get this cheaper if it is arranged as a trip.

3. StudioBinder

StudioBinder is particularly useful for learning how film techniques work. It helps you understand the difference between identifying a camera angle and explaining how that angle shapes the audience's interpretation. it provides different tips and tricks for different sectors of film making (e.g. Cinematography: camera and lighting tips). It isn't directly related to the Alevel, but some of the information on there is interesting. It's really worth checking out if you want to go into the Film industry or are interested in film-making.

YouTube Channels:

If you're looking for some Youtube channels to help you revise, below are a few I found through research for the A-Level Film Studies exams.

1. Every Frame a Painting

Every Frame a Painting provides brilliant video essays about directing, editing, visual storytelling and cinematic technique. With over 2 million subscribers, they have a wide range of videos. They have a few short films, which you could always analyse as practice. But they also have a few videos on specific techniques, such as "where to put the camera".

2. Lessons from the Screenplay

Lessons from the Screenplay explores narrative structure, character development and screenwriting. Their videos analyse movie scripts to examine exactly how and why they are so good at telling their stories. Acoording to their bio, their videos are "part educational series and part love letter to awesome films". Specifically, they have videos on symbols, subtext and themes, as well as their lessons in chronological order, which can be found in a playlist.

3. StudioBinder

StudioBinder provides explanations of cinematography, directing, editing and production. They have a wide range of videos, covering some useful content such as "9 ways to end a film" and "how to write great dialogue", which you may find beneficical for your course. There are also more "advanced techniques" explained such as directing camera movement and tutorials for some methods like "film blocking".

Top Tips:

· Learn film terminology properly.

· Analyse specific sequences. Pick important scenes from your set films and record the techniques used, their effects and their significance.

· Explain the effect of every technique. Don't simply write “a close-up is used”. Explain what it draws attention to and how it influences the audience's understanding.

· Keep a Film Studies vocabulary bank of key terminology (and definitions).

· Make a context sheet for each set film. Include its historical period, social context, production background, director and relevant cinematic movement (only if relevant to your course).

· Know the difference between plot and analysis. Retelling what happens is not the same as explaining how the film communicates meaning.

· Compare films regularly. Look at how different directors use similar techniques to communicate different ideas.

· Always mark or ask your teachers to mark your practice questions. If you went wrong, understand why and learn from your mistakes.

A* Tips:

Below, you can find a few tips to help you achieve an A or A* in Film Studies!

· Move from identification to interpretation. Naming a technique is the starting point; explaining how it constructs meaning is where your analysis becomes stronger.

· Use precise terminology.

· Consider alternative interpretations. Different audiences may interpret the same scene differently because of their experiences, expectations and cultural contexts.

· Analyse representation critically. Ask who has power, whose perspective is prioritised, what stereotypes are reinforced or challenged, and how the film encourages the audience to respond.

· Practise longer written answers.

· Always link to the audience, if possible. Explain the effect a certain technique has or what a specific camera angle draws attention to.

· Practise writing high-scoring answers under time pressure. At A* level, knowing the Film Studies isn't enough if you can't communicate it quickly and precisely.
