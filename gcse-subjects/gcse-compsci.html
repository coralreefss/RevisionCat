Top Tips:

· Don't just memorise definitions. Make sure you understand what the concept actually does.

· Practise programming regularly. These can be easy marks if you know syntax and logic rules.

· Learn the exact terminology. Computer Science mark schemes can be VERY picky.

· Practise trace tables. They are also easy marks once you're comfortable with them.

· Practise binary and hexadecimal conversions without looking at notes.

· When you lose a mark, work out why. Was it knowledge, terminology, application or exam technique?

· Don't neglect the "boring" theory. Things like legislation, networks and data representation can add up to loads of marks.

Grade 8/9 Tips:

Below, you can find a few tips to help you achieve a grade 8 or 9 ( equivalent of A or A*) in Computer Science!

· Use precise terminology. Instead of "it makes the computer faster", try "it reduces the amount of time required to process the instructions".

· Get comfortable with unfamiliar code.

· Practise application questions. A Grade 9 question might give you an unfamiliar scenario and expect you to apply something you already know.

· Don't throw away easy calculation marks. Know your conversions, binary, hexadecimal and logic gates.

· For calculations, always check units, significant figures and whether your answer is sensible.

· Do mixed topic papers reguarly.

Must-Know Information:

Below, you can find a small checklist of must-know information for GCSE Computer Science. These topics are commonly tested in the exams and may get you a few easy marks.

· The difference between hardware and software.

· The purpose of the CPU and the role of the ALU, CU and registers.

· The fetch-decode-execute cycle.

· The purpose of RAM, ROM, cache and secondary storage.

· The difference between volatile and non-volatile memory.

· Storage units, including bits, bytes, kilobytes, megabytes, gigabytes and terabytes.

· Binary, denary and hexadecimal number systems.

· Binary addition and hexadecimal conversions.

· Character encoding, including ASCII and Unicode.

· How images are represented using pixels, colour depth and resolution.

· How sound is represented using sampling rate and bit depth.

· Compression and the difference between lossy and lossless compression.

· The difference between LANs and WANs and the factors affecting network performance.

· Network topologies and transmission methods.

· The purpose of protocols such as TCP/IP, HTTP/HTTPS, FTP and DNS.

· The difference between an IP address and a MAC address.

· The purpose of routers, switches, hubs, access points and network interface cards.

· Wired and wireless networking and their advantages and disadvantages.

· Common cyber security threats, including malware, phishing, brute-force attacks and social engineering.

· Methods of protecting systems, including firewalls, encryption, strong passwords and authentication.

· The difference between authentication and authorisation.

· The difference between an algorithm and a program.

· Flowcharts, pseudocode and common programming constructs.

· Variables, constants, data types and operators.

· Sequence, selection and iteration.

· Arrays, lists, strings and records.

· Procedures, functions, parameters and return values.

· Validation, verification, authentication and testing.

· Normal, boundary, invalid and erroneous test data.

· Linear search, binary search and common sorting algorithms.

· Computational thinking, including decomposition, abstraction and pattern recognition.

· Common programming errors and how they can be identified and corrected.

· Operating systems and their main functions.

· Utility software and common utilities such as file management.

· The purpose of translators, including compilers, interpreters and assemblers.

· High-level and low-level programming languages.

· Data protection, computer misuse and other relevant computing legislation.
