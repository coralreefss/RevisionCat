Revision Methods

It is simply to provide some guidance if you are unsure where to start! There is many ways to revise that is not staring blankly at a textbook or rewriting notes. Active Recall

Active recall is a technique that involves actively stimulating your memory to retrieve information, rather than passively reviewing notes or textbooks. Active recall can be done through flashcards, self-created questions, blurting, and spaced repetition.

Spaced Repetition: review material at different intervals. For example, doing flashcards every day, then every two days, then every three days, and so on.

Best for: Memorising facts and definitions
Time needed: 15 ‑ 30 minutes
Difficulty: ⭐⭐⭐☆☆
Best subjects: Science, History, Geography, Languages, Business
Works well with: Flashcards, spaced repition and blurting.
Recommended: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Flashcards

Flashcards are a small cards used for active recall. Many students usually put a question on the front and an answer on the back. They will try to recall the answer before turning the card over.

Flashcards can be created physically using paper or card, or digitally using apps such as:

Gizmo Anki Quizlet

Personally, I preferred Gizmo when I did my GCSEs.

Best for: Learning facts and definitions
Time needed: 15 mins ‑ 1 hour
Difficulty: ⭐⭐☆☆☆
Best subjects: All subjects
Works well with: Active recall and spaced repetition
Recommended: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Blurting

Blurting is a technique used for active recall. You read about or watch a video about a topic and then write down everything you can remember on a piece of paper or a whiteboard. When you're done, use a different colour to add the information you forgot. Repeat this and eventually, all information will be in the same colour, and you can remember it.

Best for: Testing how much you really know without looking at notes.
Time needed: 20 ‑ 40 minutes
Difficulty: ⭐⭐⭐⭐☆
Best subjects: All subjects
Works well with: Active recall and past papers
Recommended: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Mindmaps

Mindmaps, or "spider diagrams" are methods of presenting information visually. If you are a visual learner, you may find this beneficial. mindmaps can be very useful to lay out information in the same place. You can create topic mindmaps or use mindmaps for blurting or active recall. However, it doesn't have to "look pretty". You will be wasting time doing elaborate titles and decoration. Try to focus on the information you are presenting.

Best for: Linking ideas, active recall, and seeing the "big picture".
Time needed: 20 mins ‑ 1 hour
Difficulty: ⭐⭐☆☆☆
Best subjects: Geography, English, Business, History, Science, Languages
Works well with: Feynman technique and blurting
Recommended: ⭐⭐⭐☆☆

Past Papers

Past papers are previous GCSE papers. They can be found on many wesbites, but my personal favourites are:

Save My Exams Revision World

Here, you can find past papers for every subject. Make sure you select the correct exam board as they are all slightly different. Many subjects should have past papers from 2017 or 2018 to the current year. You can download and print them or complete them digitally on an iPad or laptop.

Once you know the content, you should complete as many of these papers as possible. Often, exam boards like to "recycle" questions or use questions with similar wording to previous questions. If you are familiar with a question, you are more likely to get the marks!

The most effective way to use past papers is to complete them under timed conditions (e.g. 1hr 30mins for a paper that is 1hr 30mins). Then, use the mark scheme to mark it or ask your teacher to mark it for you. Go through it yourself and correct what you got wrong in a different colour. Write down the topic and revisit it by using flashcards, watching videos or any other way you prefer to revise. Then attempt the question again. Repeat this until you don't get it wrong!

Past papers are also one of the best ways to apply your content knowledge to questions. I highly recommend past papers as a revision technique.

Best for: Exam practice, time management, applying knowledge and identifying weak areas.
Time needed: 45 mins ‑ 2hours and 10mins
Difficulty: ⭐⭐⭐⭐☆
Best subjects: All subjects
Works well with: Active recall, blurting and flashcards.
Recommended: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Feynman Technique:

The Feynman Technique is a four-step learning method named after Nobel Prize-winning physicist Richard Feynman and his habit of writing out concepts in plain language to reveal what he didn't yet understand. This method is very efficient as learning by teaching deepens understanding, and self-explaination improves problem solving. Here is how it works:

1. Choose a specific concept. Pick a narrow idea, such as "Animal and Plant Cells" rather than just "Cells".

2. Explain it in simple language. Write or speak the explaination as if you're teaching a 12 year old. Try to link it to everyday examples.

3. The gaps are where you hesitate or skip steps. Note them down or highlight what you missed.

4. Return to the source only for the parts you couldn't explain. Then rewrite or say the explaination even more clearly (or simply), including analogies or examples.

Best for: Checking your understanding and finding knowledge gaps
Time needed: 20 mins ‑ 1 hour
Difficulty: ⭐⭐⭐☆☆
Best subjects: All subjects, especially maths, science, business and computer science.
Works well with: Mindmaps, Cornell Notes and active recall.
Recommended: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Cornell Notes

Cornell Notes is a method of taking structured notes to improve comprehension, organisation and review of information. Developed by Professor Walter Pauk at Cornell University, this method helps students to take more effective and easy-to-revise-from notes. Cornell notes divides the page into three main sections: a note taking column on the right, a cue column on the left for keywords or questions and a summary section at the bottom. Here's how to use Cornell Notes. This is less of a "revision method" and more of a way to take more effective notes (in class). Here's how it works:

1. During class, write the main points, facts and explainations in the note taking column. I recommend using abbriviations and symbols, such as & (and) or "b/c" (because), to save time.

2. Fill in the cue column with questions and keywords that relate to the prompts. These cues help with self-testing and active recall.

3. Write a brief summary at the bottom of the page. This can be just a sentence, but it reinforces your understanding and retention.

4. Review regularly. By covering the note-taking column and answering the cue questions you created, this will strengthen your memory and comprehension.

Best for: Organising information while learning new content.
Time needed: 20 ‑ 1 hour
Difficulty: ⭐⭐⭐☆☆
Best subjects: History, geography, English, business and science.
Works well with: Teaching someone else and active recall.
Recommended: ⭐⭐⭐⭐☆

Pomodoro Technique

The Pomodoro Technique is a time management method that uses focused work intervals followed by short breaks to boost productivity. This method was developed in the 1980s by Francesco Cirillo, who used a tomato shaped kitchen timer to commit to short but intense work sessions (which he called "pomodoros"; Italian for tomato). Here's how it works:

1. Choose a task to focus on. For example, writing an essay or watching a video.

2. Set a timer. Traditionally, it is 25 minutes for one "pomodoro", but you can set any amount of time.

3. Focused work. Concentrate soley on your task until the timer rings. Avoid distractions, such as your phone.

4. Short break. Take a 5 to 10 minute break to rest.

5. Repeat. After four pomodoros, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

I also recommend using a physical timer or a Pomodoro app to avoid checking your phone. During breaks, you could also engage in physical activity like stretching or walking rather than scrolling.

Best for: Staying focused and avoiding burnout
Time needed: Traditionally 25 minutes work and 5 minutes break, then repeat at will.
Difficulty: ⭐☆☆☆☆
Best subjects: All subjects.
Works well with: Every method! You can use Pomodoro while doing flashcards, blurting, etc..
Recommended: ⭐⭐⭐⭐☆

Works well with: Cornell notes, flashcards and active recall.

Textbooks

Textbooks can be a useful revision resource if used correctly. Staring at a page of text reading with your eyes and not your brain is not effective revision. You probably will not retain any of the information.

You can use motivation such as sweets when you reach the end of the page, but you still run the risk of not absorbing the information. You should read the text, and then quiz yourself on it after to show how much you understood and identify any gaps.

Some textbooks, such as the CPG ones, have question sections at the end. You should make the most of these and complete as many as you can!

Best for: Reviewing topics or learning new content.
Time needed: 10 ‑ 30 minutes.
Difficulty: ⭐☆☆☆☆
Best subjects: All subjects.
Works well with: Cornell notes, flashcards and blurting.
Recommended: ⭐⭐☆☆☆
