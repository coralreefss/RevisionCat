Top 3 Resources:

Below, you can find my top 3 resources for A-Level Music revision. These are resources I found through research as I did not take Music at A-Level myself. You may find other resources that are more useful for your revision!

1. Your exam board's website

Your exam board's website (which can be found above) will always be one of the most useful resources. It can tell you exactly what you're being assessed on and what the examiners will be looking for. Use the spec alongside past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports.

2. MusicTheory.net

MusicTheory.net is best for practicing technical theory that you need to be able to do rather than simply read about. The interactive exercises are particularly useful for intervals, notation, scales and key signatures.

3. Teoria

Teoria is best for ear training. This is especially useful because being able to hear intervals, chords, rhythms and tonal relationships is very different from being able to recognise them on paper.

YouTube Channels:

If you're looking for some Youtube channels to help you revise, below are a few I found through research for the A-Level Music exams.

1. David Bennett Piano

David Bennett Piano is excellent for harmony, chord progressions, theory and understanding how harmony works in actual music. He has videos explaining time signatures and useful skills across various instruments, such as how to tune a guitar and piano skills.

2. Music Matters

Music Matters provides, according to his bio, "a range of music learning resources, video courses and music exam support materials." He has many detailed videos on ear training, melody tips, harmony lessons, composing, and even provides courses and theory tests. His channel is the one I recommend the most to anyone taking A-level Music.

3. Rick Beato

Rick Beato , with over 5 million subscribers, is very useful for harmony, songwriting, production and broader musical analysis. He has hundreds of videos on scales, ear training and even music production!

4. Adam Neely

Adam Neely may be excellent for more advanced music theory, rhythm, harmony and contemporary musical ideas. He provides lessons for bass guitar and scale challenges, as well as a tutorial to "get good at music". While his channel may not be specifically A-level targeted, some of the videos may be useful to you (especially if you play bass!)

5. 12tone

12tone is great for musical analysis and seeing how analytical ideas can be applied to real pieces. They have some videos on scales and understanding music itself.

Top Tips:

· Don't just search the generic "A-level Music revision". Instead, search for the specific thing you don't understand: "secondary dominants explained" or "A-level four-part harmony". You'll find more useful resources that way.

· Practise little and often. Music is one of those subjects where 20 minutes of regular practice can be more useful than one enormous revision session.

· Listen actively. Don't just have music playing in the background. Listen specifically for harmony, melody, rhythm, texture, instrumentation, structure and tonality.

· Learn terminology through sound. If you learn "suspension", actually listen to examples of suspensions.

· Make an error log. Record recurring mistakes rather than simply recording your percentage.

· For composition, experiment early. Don't leave your composition until the deadline and then try to invent everything at once.

· Know your scales and keys automatically. You don't want to spend valuable analysis time working out basic key signatures.

· Always mark or ask your teachers to mark your practice questions. If you went wrong, understand why and learn from your mistakes.

A* Tips:

Below, you can find a few tips to help you achieve an A or A* in Music!

· Know exactly what you're hearing. At the top level, don't settle for "there is a chord change". Identify the relevant harmonic, melodic, rhythmic, textural or structural feature and be sure you can explain its effect.

· Use precise terminology. "It sounds sad" is an observation; explaining the "minor tonality" or another relevant feature gives you something you can actually analyse.

· Make connections across musical elements. For example, explain how instrumentation and texture reinforce a particular harmonic or structural idea.

· Train your ears under pressure. Give yourself short listening extracts and identify as many musical features as possible within a time limit.

· For composition, understand your intention. Be able to explain what you wanted the piece to communicate and how your musical choices achieve that.

· Record practice performances and critique them objectively. You could try to write down 2 or 3 specific improvements
